# Freedom Force — IGN Review

- Source: [IGN](https://www.ign.com/articles/2002/05/28/freedom-force-review)
- Author: Joseph Antolick
- Published: 2002-05-28

Within the computer realm, it is undeniable that worlds based on variations of high fantasy, mixing magic and medieval weaponry with races such as elves and dwarves, provide by far the most frequent type of setting for role-playing games.  Science fiction has also proven to be fertile ground, providing distant planets to explore, usually populated with imaginative alien creatures.  Having ventured into the latter with the critically acclaimed System Shock 2, developer Irrational Games has tackled the challenge of a different theme, one that may be especially intriguing due to its infrequent use to date.

   Freedom Force brings together elements of squad-based strategy and RPG-style character development in a setting inspired both visually and thematically by the 1960s and Silver Age comics.  The game offers both single- and multiplayer modes, with the former having a storyline in which you must save the planet from mysterious, evil aliens.  And for those who are so inclined, it also enables players to create their own super-powered heroes or villains.  In this hands-on Freedom Force Review, we take a look at this very interesting title to help you decide if it's one that fits your playing preferences.

 The first task of every roleplaying game is to draw the player into an alternate world. Maybe it will be a realm of swords and sorcery, filled with knights, elves, and all the rest of the fantasy cliches many have grown to love. Maybe it will be a post-apocalyptic Earth, with once-familiar cities and societies now burned, irradiated, or otherwise changed into uncharted territory. On the PC in particular, role-playing games have had diverse settings to say the least - Any given number of alternate universes, alien worlds, and undiscovered realms have been invaded or protected by gamers over the years. With this sort of competition in mind, one of the hardest challenges any new game has is to be, in a word, fresh. To offer gamers a world that will be truly unfamiliar to them, instead of just a variation on revisited theme.

   Enter Freedom Force, Irrational Games' strategic role-playing foray into a world of comic book heroism and villainy. Earth is experiencing some trying times - the Cold War is starting to get into full swing, with communism rearing it's ugly head even in the streets of all-American Liberty City. But this becomes the least of worries when an alien overlord bent on conquering the universe hatches a fiendish plot to imbue the worst examples of mankind with the awe-inspiring powers of Energy X, and thereby condemn humanity to destroy itself. Luckily, Earth receives a chance to avoid this fate when the canisters are instead scattered randomly throughout Liberty City, not only spawning twisted fiends but upstanding heroes with the power to defeat them. Thus begins the mission of the Freedom Force to protect the world from whatever evil may seek to threaten it.

   Players won't need to wait for the first canister of Energy X to fall to see just how heavily the game borrows from the Silver Age era of comic books. The influence isn't just hinted at, but out and out admitted to, from the box the game ships in right to the title screen before it begins. The character designs, the campy dialogue and storyline, even the look of the word bubbles are reminiscent of a long-gone comic age, and they make for a surprisingly fresh world to play in. Players who are comic fans will find some obvious parallels between their favorite heroes and villains and the ones that make up the cast of Freedom Force. But even gamers who have managed never to hear the name Captain America won't end up feeling lost - the characters are genuinely interesting, even endearing, and the game's world is compelling all on its own.

   While the campy, yet straight-played world of Freedom Force gives gamers a refreshing backdrop, what really brings the title to life is the quality of both the graphics and the sound. The many areas in the game all have been created with a surprising attention to detail. Locales are filled with many different kinds of objects - from traffic lights and hot dog carts to alien weapons and fighter jet planes. Each item has a bit of text associated with it for added flavor - Superman fans should get a chuckle out of the phonebooth's description - along with more vital information such as weight, and the amount of damage it can take. Both statistics are factors in the game owing to the fact that just about everything can be destroyed or used somehow. Entire buildings can be made to collapse if enough damage is inflicted upon them, and the remaining chunks of rubble can be lifted and hurled by friend or foe alike. It's this kind of interactivity that makes Freedom Force's world really seem alive, creating authentic comic book moments for the player. Battles can be decided with a swing of a lamp post, the throw of a car, or the use of anything else one's chosen hero can get his or her hands on.

 The art and objects of Freedom Force may create a lush staging ground for the game's many battles, and the sound completes the task of drawing players in. The voice acting is top-notch for the most part, with the narrator's voice in particular a perfect match for the mood and attitude of the game's characters and battlefields. The sound effects are of the same calibre, clear and appropriate, from the shot of a handgun to the rumble of a building as it comes tumbling to the ground. But what really stands out is the music - mostly simple, effective tracks, but all matched perfectly to their scenes. Stage themes build further on the personalities of the characters, from the ominous theme of Nuclear Winter to El Diablo's more jazzy tune. Freedom Force's music is repetitive at times, with many tracks seeming to be variations on a basic theme. But this is a minor issue in what is otherwise one of the game's strong suits.

   While Freedom Force makes the grade and then some in all of these areas, a few pitfalls are encountered where actual gameplay is concerned. Exploration and combat takes place in real time for the most part - click on a character, then click on their destination to move there. If you wish to attack an enemy, use a power or affect the surroundings, just click on the enemy or object and choose an option from the presented menu. The default option of the game is to pause whenever an action menu is opened, easing the burden of having to make too many decisions too quickly, but it's still easy to feel overwhelmed.  Your squad of heroes can get divided up over several screens, creating the need to pause repeatedly to make sure no part of the battle is being ignored. At times, Freedom Force plays more like a nerve-wracking turn-based than a real-time one, since it's rare to be able to sit back and watch the action after deciding on a strategy.  Instead, pausing and pausing again is needed to give additional orders and take care of every member of your force. As well, projectile attacks are carried out in a way that can be frustrating; many times a bolt will hit the terrain, but no indication is given as to whether this was caused by an error in lining up the shot or a lack of skill in the ability.

   In between missions, the player is able to direct the development of the Freedom Force characters. Levels are gained by taking part in the missions, and as they are reached, points are accumulated. These can then be spent towards enhancing existing powers or purchasing new ones, and the various heroes and heroines have a remarkable array of abilities at their disposal. Each character gained throughout the storyline has its own strengths and weaknesses, and can be developed in different ways. For example, you'll have to decide between investing in the Minuteman's patriot whirl, a circular attack that strikes every foe around the hero, or the simple smash which only hits one character, but has a chance to stun.

   It's a long, hard quest to save the world, but Freedom Force offers a few extras to keep players interested for longer than the single-player storyline. For one thing, many of the game's levels have hidden and secondary objectives. Most of these aren't hard to find, and require only a more thorough search of the level before completion. Ferreting out these hidden events not only rewards the player with more opportunities to rid the world of evil, but they also help rack up experience and prestige for further training. Not only that, but there is also a built-in hero construction kit for players who wish to create their own customized superheroes. While most may choose simply to use the pre-made character designs and powers, Irrational included the ability to further personalize a character, complete with downloadable meshes and skins from their website.  Multiplayer capability is also included, with teams of heroes facing of against each other - relatively basic, but still a welcome addition to the single-player game.

 The Last Word

 Freedom Force is a truly original game, one that stands out among the rest of the role-playing titles for the PC. While the gameplay has some minor flaws, the amount of effort Irrational put into this game was apparent the moment I loaded it up.  The title's superhero theme may be its most obvious feature, but what drew me and kept me playing was the richness of it all, from the art to the story and music. This is the sort of game I play not because the combat or the quests are so exciting, but because I want to see more of the world, all the way to the very end - a well-crafted game overall.
