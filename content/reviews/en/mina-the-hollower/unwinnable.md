# My Toxic Relationship with Mina the Hollower’s Giant Hammer — unwinnable Review

- Source: [unwinnable](https://unwinnable.com/2026/07/31/my-toxic-relationship-with-mina-the-hollowers-giant-hammer/)
- Author: Jack Dunn
- Published: 2026-07-31


Games can be distilled into a common language, a through-line connecting every sentence of dialogue, setpiece, and gameplay mechanic.

For Yacht Club’s  Mina the Hollower , that language is frenetic movement. The 2D Zelda-inspired adventure is an elaborate fetch quest with the titular mouse re-powering the generators of the island nation of Ossex. Everything is tied to this sense of constant forward motion, from enemies constantly trying to knock you off into bottomless pits, to the gigantic bosses that eclipse half the screen, to level design made up entirely of shifting platforms and projectiles whipping around Mina in a maelstrom of pixels.

But what happens if there’s a core mechanic that’s completely at odds with that tone? The answer is that you may have an “easy” time playing the game even though you feel chastised for doing so.

Enter Mina’s hammer. At the outset of  Mina the Hollower , the player has an option between three weapons: a pair of daggers, a mace and chain, and the aforementioned giant hammer. The first two weapons fit into the playstyle that I feel like most players would gravitate towards – choosing speed over maximum damage, striking at enemies fast while dodging them using the game’s central burrowing mechanic.

The hammer is completely at odds with this playstyle, requiring a good few seconds to line up a swing that delivers any significant amount of damage. Faster swings that do much less damage feel like wasted time. What could be a balanced equation of movement and damage instead becomes a simple timing exercise of when to wind up a swing in the right spot. Using the hammer in a game focused on frenetic movement felt like I was giving Mina the silent treatment.

But I wanted to keep shunning whatever the game was shouting at me. Using Mina’s hammer makes me feel like I am bending the game’s language to my own vernacular. A well-timed swing can dispatch smaller enemies with ease, and even while balancing swings and desperately dodging projectiles was nearly impossible, I loved the dance it created. Every time I tried to switch to another weapon it felt like weaning off of a drug, a luxury that I just couldn’t quit.

It’s certainly not the first time I have felt like I was playing a game obtusely on purpose. Lots of commercially successful games don’t exist in a spectrum where there’s room for such stark differences in play. There are clear outlines for what the player-character is supposed to be doing at any given moment, which tools you need to complete a certain task, and where your skill level needs to be at.

Games like  Mina  don’t have those guardrails. Developer Yacht Club explicitly outlined that there are no collectibles in the game that are locked behind progression flags, no bosses that you need to beat before moving on to any areas in the map. Players can go to what reviewers consider the sixth and seventh bosses far before they are “supposed to”, but they will be challenged.  Mina  is a hard game for a reason.

Even though its difficulty and gameplay are singular,  Mina  and its challenging gameplay has the unfortunate fate of being lumped in with the soulslikes of the world. The comparisons are not unwarranted – bosses reward skill expression and precise inputs, while Mina’s healing mechanic is directly tied to the amount of damage she deals. These mechanics mirror games like  Dark Souls  almost to a T.

There was another reason for comparison for me, personally. To inform my first  Dark Souls  playthrough, I took to the internet and its  Dark Souls  character build guides. My favorite was the “bonk” build, a playstyle where player-characters only focus on strength, HP, and wielding the biggest hammer they can get their massive hands on. It bears a striking resemblance to  Mina ’s hammer – not just because of the size of the weapon, but the ability to “cheese” the game with seemingly less skill expression.

But that resemblance does not mean  Dark Souls  and  Mina  are identical.  Mina ’s damage has more hard caps compared to the variability of  Dark Souls ’ stats. Mina can also upgrade the hammer to do more damage if wound up for longer, and also gets an add-on spin attack that can dispatch enemies not in her direct line of fire. All of these require mobility without the weapon in hand, and Mina is far more mobile than a  Dark Souls  player character often is.

The balance that  Mina  tries to strike with the hammer is one that relies on player patience. Patience is in short supply in  Mina the Hollower , as one does not simply linger around when an enemy could knock you off the closest platform at any moment. But patience comes in different styles. The majority of the patience in  Mina the Hollower  is active: circling enemies like a shark while they shoot a million projectiles at you, burrowing under said projectiles, and waiting for the right moment to strike. The hammer requires complete patience – active and passive. Hammers don’t get swung without time to swing them. It forces the player to sit in this uncomfortable world and bet on their own skills to hit something.

The main thing I have learned from actually playing through  Mina the Hollower , instead of thinking about playing it, is that it really does not matter how each individual person approaches said game. Much like  Dark Souls , the game is about skill expression – there are countless gadgets to experiment with, both in Mina’s mobility and her combat. Or, you could just disregard everything else that’s handed to you and play how you like. I feel like playing with the hammer.

The hammer feels inevitable. When I miss, it’s on me for not waiting long enough. How toxic is it to wait around for something that you know will come through for you? How wrong am I actually playing this game? The answer, for me, is not at all.
