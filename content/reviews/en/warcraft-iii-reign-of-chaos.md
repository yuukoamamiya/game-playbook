# Warcraft III: Reign of Chaos — IGN Review

- Source: [IGN](https://www.ign.com/articles/2001/12/06/warcraft-iii-reign-of-chaos-3)
- Author: IGN Staff
- Published: 2001-12-06

E3 2001

  So there's this monkey man in India you see... Oh wait, yeah, you want to hear about that game from Blizzard with the orcs and the fighting... allright. WarCraft III is looking pretty good and getting pretty close to a point where it may actually have the chance of releasing this winter like they're promising. Even though we got the chance to see it not too long ago, the temptation to check this one out was too great, mainly because I wasn't the one that got to see it last and I got a chance to check out a bunch of the really cool new ideas and units that will make the races very different.

 But guess what? So did that damn Vincent guy in his pre-E3 blowout of the game. Damn him. So if you want to know about Night Elves, mana stones, creeps, and the camera system, go to the last preview, otherwise stick around and here a couple of the neato tricks that some of the heroes can pull off.

 Anyway so like check out the WarCraft III trailer. It's in Bink format and it's 30 MBs.

 As you know, heroes play a pretty central role in Blizzard RTSs, but for most part have been relegated to the middle of the bases where nothing could hurt them. This was mainly because missions revolved around keeping these hero units alive. They're going to change this around a little bit with the ability to resurrect dead heroes. This should allow players the opportunity to take their heroes out into battle without the worry of losing them. And this is a very cool thing because the heroes all have some pretty cool looks, attacks, and spells that can be gained through the advancement of levels.

 All of the heroes are denoted through the glowing weapons that they possess and look very unique compared to the rest of the units. Right now there are four heroes for each of the races with four abilities that can be used. This still could be changed around a little bit, but so far this is the best balance for the gameplay. Heroes also have four inventory slots that can be filled by finding special items around the map, or purchasing things from stores that are scattered around the maps.

 As the heroes gain levels, they also have the opportunity to put a point into their abilities. Some can't be used until they reach a certain level. One of the heroes that I saw had a couple of pretty cool special abilities. One of them allows him to drop a little flag on the ground that cloaks everything in a certain radius around that flag, while another of them gave him the ability to resurrect dead monsters. The resurrected won't stay around forever, but you can do this to enemy units as well so taking one of their powerful units can really help quite a bit even if you only have them for a little while. Each of the heroes will have abilities that suit their races like the one of the undead heroes that can raise skeletons.

 Even though its still a ways away from its final release, the game actually looks like its going to be pretty entertaining. While this is certainly a far cry from their original RPS idea, the addition of the hero units will make things more interesting. They're still debating whether to let heroes advance from level to level due to experience issues, which I really hope that they do, but either way, it should still please the countless WarCraft fans out there.

 -- Dan Adams

 April 26, 2001

 We're going to cut through the cake and give you filling this time around, since you know all you need to about Warcraft III generalities. It's hard to believe that there are more details to discuss about lovely WCIII, but believe me, there are. Night Elves, more hero spells, dynamic maps, camera control, and a peek at the Undead beauties, just for starters.

 Right now the Blizzard employees are working weekends and long hours to prepare for this year's E3 demo, and it looks like they'll be busy at this year's show demonstrating two fully functional races (Human and Orc), most of the Night Elves, and a small peek at the Undead. At this point, the Night Elves are still being implemented into the game, and the Undead are only present as a select set of animated characters, but not the full building tree. Quests haven't been touched at this point, since that's one of the final steps in finishing the game. In classic Blizzard style, the bulk of the company's brainpower is being dedicated to testing out multiplayer and balancing out the races in battle. The team just finished its first four player game, and things are moving at a steady pace. There's a long road ahead for development, but we've got more reasons why you're going to shut up, wait, and like it.

   Magic Among the Creeps

 Something that the team hasn't really talked about are the Mana stones, another resource on top of your standard gold resource, and an interesting addition to the single and multiplayer game. Mana stones allow you to purchase new heroes and high-end upgrades, and are severely limited on the map. You'll only get them through killing "creeps," the NPC monsters that are spread out on the map. Mana stones become critical to getting some of the more powerful additions to your town and heroes, so it will serve you well to search out the map and find the creeps before your enemy does. It also stops the base-to-base battles that become the norm on multiplayer maps, because you'll be spending a bulk of time actually exploring areas of the map just to look for additional Mana stones. Producer Rob Pardo also mentioned that this helps with the dreaded opening minutes of RTS games, where you're quickly building your base in preparation for the oncoming attack. Instead, you'll be spending those first initial minutes exploring your nearby territory, getting the precious mana stones, and looking for resources.

 Speaking of creeps, we were able to take a look at some of the new beasts that you'll be slaying in your journey towards world dominance. While Pardo breezed through some of the latest additions, I managed to catch a Junk Golem, looking suspiciously like a mech, attack some elves, and a few ogres wander the open roads. There are 25 models at this point, with 100 different creeps thanks to the tweaking of the strengths and abilities of each model. Just as in Diablo II, you'll fight many variants of the same creature, some with different strengths, skills, and spells to use. You'll even see some familiar Diablo boss creeps that will have the ability to resurrect other creeps. On a related note, you'll also be able to kill wandering sheep just for kicks, so that's saying something.

 Pointy Ears and Smelly Jerks

 So let's talk Night Elves -- they're fast, they're cool, and the women disappear at night. The elves are a long range race, where even the grunts are archers. You'll have to use stealth and cunning, not to mention a lot of run and gun tactics to beat your opponents.  While I didn't get a chance to see one in motion, the elven township can literally uproot itself, travel to a new area, and even attack due to the fact that all elven buildings are actually Treants, living trees. As you'd expect, the Elves are tied to the forest, so a lot of their power and abilities are related to the trees themselves. For instance, the Keeper of the Grove, an elven hero, can use the spell Nature's Touch to destroy or regrow trees at will, allowing you to use a forest as a sort of movable doorway for your troops. The Sentinel unit also uses nature to her aid in the form of an owl that can be sent out to scout out territory quickly. Female units like the Assassin, Sentinel, and the Archer also have another advantage -- cloaking. At night they'll turn completely invisible when still, allowing you to sneak up on an enemy without their knowledge, as long as you're not caught in mid-movement. Of course, if you build up a hero like Anara Chillwind's spells, you'll also be able to unlock the 5th level ultimate spell Twilight, which will turn day to night, and turn the map into your personal playground.

 I managed to get Pardo to show off some Undead characters, though they aren't quite up to speed at this point. Still, I had a chance to see a couple of the characters, like the smelly, fly filled, noxious Abomination, a sort of Frankenstein monster that can use his gas and his size to smash the world under his thumb. A Death Knight galloped on a flowing purple cloud, and a Lich moved through trails on a set of flippery, flowery legs. The Lich will ultimately launch cold spells, culminating with summoning a giant Frost Worm, but at this point it looked surly and stared back at us. The last, and most interesting peek was the Necromancer, who will work similarly to Diablo II, taking the skeletons of dead enemies and friends and turning them into skeleton warriors and archers, using our favorite spell, good old Corpse Explosion. What's interesting is that bodies actually decay before becoming skeletons, starting out as fleshy corpses. This is important, because Ghouls can get HP back by "cannibalizing" fleshy corpses and turning them into skeletons. As part of this grisly circle of life, the Banshee can also lend a hand in the process by using one of her songs, Dirge, to keep the fleshy corpses preserved until the Ghouls come to feed, and then the Necromancers follow to enlist for the army. It's a mana-draining spell however, and it's ranged, so you'll have a limited space and time to use her powers before the corpses begin to decay naturally.

 Slopes and the Intimate Camera

 The camera seems to have been pulled out even more, and it's nearly identical to the 2D Warcraft at this point. Since you've already swallowed the fact that this isn't 3D in Blizzard's original sense, you shouldn't be all that surprised. The high angle makes it easier to see your units, and as such, also allows for larger parties again, as well as the disappearance of formal parties altogether. You'll once again have full freedom to combine heroes in parties, grunt rush, and mix and match as you like. At this point there's  a soft limit of nine members total to a group, though that's far from a formal number. You'll have a 75 food cap though, with units using 1, and heroes using 5, so you can expect the general limits present from earlier Blizzard RTS titles. You will have a modicum of camera control, though, so wipe those tears, little soldier. The mouse wheel (and some keyboard equivalent) will allow you to zoom in closer to get a better view of the action, but the camera angle will be handled by the computer. Still, it will give you a chance to see the Tauren Chieftain slam his fist into a poor Elven Archer's pretty face.

 The graphics are almost identical to the version we saw months ago, with the most major differences being the addition of new sloping terrain to add to the 3D feel of the environments, as well as rivers and ponds. Because it's 3D all of the elements have an effect on the game, and while the team is still talking about the effect of, say, firing arrows off a hill, the plan is to have it affect range, accuracy, and line of sight, for starters. The 3D elements will also come to play with the rivers as well, with shallower sections that can be crossed, and other deep pools that will be unpassable by normal ground units.

 What else, what else? I got a peek at the Altar, a mid-level building that will revive your dead heroes at a significant mana cost, and I had a chance to see the Mercenary camp, where you can purchase additional neutral units to help you in battle. Pardo showed off the seasonal changes you can expect in the maps, and mentioned that trees will be used to separate areas of the map, just as in WC II. So much information, so little time. One month, and you'll get even more details on Warcraft III, so rest easy and spend your time thinking about how you're going to sneak your Night Elves into the backside of an Orc camp. There'll be a test in May.

 -- Vincent Lopez

  October 26, 2000

 The last time we visited our hero Warcraft III, it was in a state of mental and emotional crisis. Camera changes, gameplay tweaks, and rumors of simplification were turning what started out as the new name in strategy gaming (literally -- Role Playing Strategy was coined by Blizzard for WCIII) into Warcraft in 3D. The good news is, it's still Warcraft, in 3D. The better news is that it looks as if Blizzard is still on track to deliver some major changes to its platinum strategy formula, from what we've seen from Blizzards visit to our offices today. But don't believe for a second that the controversy's still not about to run strong with some of the latest developments from the Blizzard camp.

 Bill Roper, Senior Director of Developer Relations for Blizzard, lays out the changes, reworkings, and tweaks (and untweaks) of Warcraft III simply. The original goal for the game was to create a game that really blurred the line between strategy games and RPGs by focusing on quests, characters, and most importantly, keeping the focus on smaller groups than the anonymous focus of WC I & II. After bringing in the camera, and implementing many of the changes, the team began to feel that Warcraft III was beginning to become "a cool RPG with strategy elements," as Roper puts it. So the camera was pulled back, some of the town building elements were tweaked to become more similar to the other Warcraft games, and other changes were implemented to ensure the "we emphasize the S, not the RP."

 This, of course, caused no end to the letters, newsgroup posts, and critical rumors that Warcraft III was being pulled back into simply a 3D version of the first two games, with some minor additions. Fortunately, it seems that Blizzard is back on track, and still delivering a new strategy gaming experience with its latest build of the title. Hero units are intact, interactive environments are there, the quests is still in place, and the five-race structure is coming along well.

 Yes, the camera is pulled out a bit. But not to the extent of Blizzard's other titles (or the upcoming Westwood game Emperor: Battle for Dune, for that matter), and it's still close enough to give much more detail to the world of Warcraft and its characters. The structure reflects the more intimate settings, as well. While Warcraft had a mixture of vehicles, troops, ships, and gadgetry, Warcraft III brings the world down to a more human level. Because the levels are smaller in scope, with larger characters, you'll have less units to deal with as a whole, but each will be much more vital to your success. Roper talked about the grunt rush philosophy, and hopes that Warcraft III will focus more on the strategy tricks that really get fans exchanging battle stories, instead of tossing 30 units at a base and watching it crumble. "The vast majority of combat is 'how do I use these six guys,'" said Roper about the tighter focus of the game.

 This also plays into resource management as well. The team is still committed to making sure that unlike most RTS games, where 70% of your time is spent handling resources, that you'll be spending that same percentage on battle in WCIII, with only 30% on town and resource management. Part of that has to do with the story-based resource structure, where you'll rescue a mine from monsters to get access to the gold. Part of that has to do with the unique resources for the races. We've talked about it before, but Blizzard reemphasized the importance of resources in WCIII. Humans and Orcs use gold, for instance, but the undead use dead bodies, meaning that on an average map, you may not have to worry about someone getting to an mine source before you do. You may have better things to worry about, such as not letting any of your uniques bite the big one in the presence of the less-than-alive.

 I was most excited to hear that the Hero units are coming along nicely, and are definitely still in place to give gamers the biggest change to the Blizzard strategy structure. Each race will have six to eight different heroes that you'll be able to use upon building a specific structure, each with a randomly generated unique name, and each with the ability to hold items, gain levels, and learn special spells and skills a la' a Diablo-esque tree system. Normal units have attack and defense ratings, but Hero units also have strength, agility, and intelligence stats that affect attack and movement speed, attack strength, and total mana power and regeneration speed. Your Heroes will be able to grow in strength and skill over the course of missions, but unlike the specialized characters in Starcraft, you'll be able to get them back after their death by paying a healthy toll at their structure (which varies depending on the race).

 Roper showed us an Orc heroes special ability, the Raging Scream, which allowed all nearby units to move at quicker speeds for a limited time, and showed off some human powers such as mass teleportation. Another change -- you won't have to attach units to a hero in order to have them benefit from the Hero's special ability now. As in Diablo, each specific Hero unit has their own set of special spells and abilities that you can choose from as they level up, and it's up to you to decide whether to create a character with a wide range of spells, or just a few incredibly strong ones. Heroes will still be able to hold items, and the team is still putting together how you'll find those items in the game. So far, it looks as if some of the options will be in stores in particular levels, from solving specific quests, or by defeating particular heroes. You'll have access to special armor, special weapons, and other gadgets and gizmos that will boost your Hero's stats, or protect them from specific attacks. You've played Diablo II, so you know just how good Blizzard is at those fantastic stat-boosting items.

 Speaking of quests, you can rest assured that that's in place as well. While I didn't get to see an actual scenario, Roper ran me through some of the ideas they're planning for the campaign structure. Like Homeworld, missions will begin with one task, but quickly branch off as you reach specific locations, talk to characters, or accomplish specific tasks. For instance, you might be called on to find a mercenary camp. Once you find the camp, however, you'll be given more quests to accomplish, like rescuing a mine from beasts in order to gain access to the resources, or intercepting a caravan in order to steal a key to gain access to the back entrance of an Orc camp. Did I mention the caravan had been ransacked by Harpies before you could get there? The team's working on making campaigns more than just one simple task, and so far the ideas are compelling.

 Last but certainly not least, Roper mentioned some of the multiplayer plans that they have in store for the game as well. Years of feedback from Starcraft have proven on thing -- people wanna destroy. Capture the Flag and Greed modes are virtually unused in comparison to the straightforward two on two or three on three top and bottom map battles. Fans love big battles, so WCIII is definitely looking at multiplayer with that in mind. For instance, you'll be able to use NPC monsters to fill a map for extra fighting, or just to stop senseless grunt rushes within the first few minutes by having your competition fight through random beasts before they can reach your base.

 Also interesting are the plans to have the races play off each other in multiplayer games. For instance Orc spells might affect the undead differently than the living, and so on, so good Orc players may specifically want to play with another race that works well with their playing style. Not only will you want to pick a teammate this time around, but you'll want to find someone that fits well with your race, and playing style as well.

 Pretty great, right? So what's this about controversy? I haven't really mentioned the camera yet. Blizzard has decided at this point that you won't be able to move the camera in gameplay, either by zooming in, or by rotating the camera around the scene. The team cites that gameplay is the focus for WC III, as with all Blizzard titles, and that the 3D moving camera confused and cluttered the gameplay. The camera will still rotate, zoom, and lower for cutscenes, however, so in terms of story, 3D will be utilized to its full extent. You just won't be able to zoom in to particular battles anymore, or spin things around to get an alternate view.

 Telling someone that there'll be 3D, but that they won't be able to touch the camera in 3D is like telling a five year old that he can stare at cookies in a jar, but he better not touch. Fans will no doubt freak out at the prospect of being unable to zoom in to a specific battle, or rotate the camera to get an alternate view of a situation . The question is, will you put your faith in the same company that brought you Starcraft, Diablo, and Warcraft? Of course you will. Blizzard has historically held gameplay in the highest regard, and we're hoping that while these changes will make Warcraft slightly less visually dynamic, it will ultimately make for a much more rewarding overall product. Besides, if there's one thing the company has on its side, it's time. Warcraft III was and is still slated for release sometime next year.

 -- Vincent Lopez

 May 16, 2000

 E3 2000 Update

 One of the most crowded booths on the floor this year was one of the games that we know you guys were really looking forward to hearing more about. The Warcraft series is by far one of the most successful properties in the gaming industry and it looks like the newest addition to the line, Warcraft III, is going to keep that rampant popularity going. There have been quite a few changes from the time that Blizzard announced that they were making the game. Actually, there have been a few notable changes just in the time that has passed since we last got a look at it.

 So let's jump into the most blaring one that you may have heard about. Blizzard has announced a new race for the game called the undead and it was in the version that was displayed on the show floor. The other announcement that accompanied this one was that Blizzard had decided to eliminate one of the races from the game leaving it with five races. There is still one more to be announced and they are pretty tight lipped about it at the moment. I tried to trick them into telling me a couple of times, but alas, I am not the talented trickster that I would like to be.

 Okay, back to the undead. They are a whole different race in terms of unit production, resource management, and building. Unlike the orcs and humans, the undead can only have one town on a map. This may seem like a huge disadvantage as they won't be able to make any forward bases, but when you think about it, it isn't really important for the undead to do that. Why? Because the undead don't need to mine gold to build units, that's why. All of the resources for the undead come in the form of corpses and mana. Units are created by using spells to reanimate corpses. There may be some variation on that though as a developer told me that they have considered having multiple existing units mashed together to form other creatures like the Abomination hero unit. This also has it's advantages and disadvantages. The main advantage to it is that you can create units wherever you have a spellcaster that has the ability to create units. The bad part is that you have to kill things before you can do this because you need the corpses. The town for the undead is a bit different than that of the other races as well. Instead of having only buildings, of which there are a few, spells that take a long time to cast will open up the ability to create new units. The spell will remain visible in the town though. One of them was green swirling fog type effect that symbolized that banshees could now be created. There were plenty of units to look at on the undead side such as Pool Wraiths, Abominations, Anti-Paladins, Lichs, Dreadlords, Deathknights, Crypt Fiends, Banshees, and a huge creature called the Frost Wyrm.

 Allright enough of that, on to the other changes that you'll be really interested in. Remember that the game was originally touted as the first real role playing strategy game? Well it seems to be moving farther and farther away from that type of gameplay each time we see it. Now don't get me wrong, there are still role playing elements in the game such notably in the heroes and they're ability to gain levels. They have been moving the camera around trying to decide what they wanted best and what they settled on was a three quarter view that really lends itself towards a strategy feel in the game. What this also allows is the non-hero units to walk around without having to be attached to a hero unit and gave the artists a chance to focus on creating quality art for a fixed camera view. The camera angle used to be closer to the ground which really did give the game more of a role playing feel. The only time that the camera will be moved is when it zooms out for big spells that need the extra space to really appreciate it.

 There will still be quests in the game though. So there still is more of a role playing element then there was in the first two Warcraft games. But the emphasis is on strategy now. Larger amounts of troops will be able to be created than they were originally planning but not nearly as many as Starcraft. They are thinking about having around 75 troops as the limit.

 As far as multiplayer goes, they are still trying to decide exactly how that's going to work. One of the developers of the game did say that they "are designing units with multiplayer in mind." One of the big things that they are trying to work out with the multiplayer is the interaction with the different races. They are trying to make it so that the special things can happen when different races ally with each other. Whether that means special attacks or special kinds of formations, they aren't really saying, but it certainly peaked my interest.

 The game really does look pretty at this point in the development and we can only look forward to more neat stuff as nobody has seen the Daemon units yet. Apparently they are still in the concept stage for the most part, so there won't be any screens of them for a while. So all in all, I'm a little disappointment about the pullback of the RPG element of the game and the reversion to the older interface type. But the game really does look good and will probably add enough new gameplay to the RTS genre that it will generate the same fanatical following that the other two Warcraft games and Starcraft have inspired in people.

 -- Dan Adams

 February 12, 1999

 While I was at Blizzard checking out Diablo II on Wednesday, I just happened to sneak a peek at Warcraft III and talked with Producer Rob Pardo about some of the progress that's been made on the game. Visually it continues to shine, fleshing out the Warcraft world and giving it all the justice it deserves -- but it's the ideas involved with the development right now that are the most exciting, especially when you see the company once again breaking into new territory. The animation in place for some of the races and the world, though limited to two small areas at this point, already proves just how successful the leap to 3D has been.

 Right now the design team is focusing on the foundation of Warcraft III's gameplay -- the units. The hero units are the most important addition to the Warcraft universe, and exemplify the new RPS (Role Playing Strategy) philosophy that Blizzard is promoting. Like Tal mentioned in his earlier preview (just scroll down the page a bit to read it), hero units will carry-on throughout the game, gaining experience, abilities and levels as the game progresses. The key to having specialized heroes such as the Arch-Mage or the Paladin is that not only will they have the ability to gain levels, cast more spells and carry an inventory of items, but they'll be characters within the world that you can grow attached to, something that hasn't really been given much thought in the strategy genre. Pardo mentioned more than once on my visit the need for a universe that gamers could feel a part of, like classic RPGs, without having to sacrifice the Warcraft flavor. For Pardo, creating the perfect mix of connection and depth while making sure that you don't get bogged down in pure RPGing is key to the game's success. For instance, though they would like players to choose how stats are changed by leveling up a character, they're unsure as to how much control a gamer will have over every individual stat. The game is still steeped in strategy, and the team is trying to avoid anything that might pull gamers out of that broad brand of action. The same goes for leadership points, the statistic that covers how many units a particular hero can attach to them when in battle. Warcraft was built on large groups taking each other out in battle, but WIII is more about parties, and the leaders that will guide the smaller parties through specific goal-oriented tasks. It may be useful to allow gamers to build up the leadership levels in characters over time, but if it proves to bog down the focus of the game, then they might just decide to give different heroes different set leadership skills, or just simplify the process of setting stats altogether. Though the amount of units you control are much less than in Blizzard's other RPGs, you'll still be controlling multiple teams simultaneously, which is exactly why the team is wary about getting too involved with tweaking every individual character.

 Of course, it doesn't hurt that the new storyline is becoming its own little fantasy soap opera. Take a look at the WIII trailer (25.5MB EXE), and you'll realize just how this new game really will be from its predecessors in terms of style and storytelling. With six different races involved, each with individual skills and slants, you'd be right to assume that the game is taking a page from another Blizzard classic, Starcraft. A large story will link all of the races together, and by playing each race through, you'll get a different section of the larger adventure. In-game cutscenes will push each individual mission forward, as well as RPG elements such as a broad range of NPCs that will give you key information, or tasks to accomplish within a certain mission.

 The largest problem with introducing heroes, deeper NPC characters, or anything other than a couple of mission objectives and some targets to pummel, was that you frankly just didn't have the time to deal with it. Pardo cites this problem as the Peon Train: having to spend what he felt was 70% of your time on resource management for about 30% of time on some heavy action. This time around, the goal is to flip that percentage over, and part of that process is tossing out the old resource management guidebook altogether. Instead of sending off peons to gather resources, you'll encounter resources within specific missions that you must gain access to in order to build units and buildings. He illustrated this by showing a mine that had been ransacked by Orcs and Minotaurs, where a human promised that upon return of a special object, that the mine could be repaired. Once the mine was repaired, than the gold level began to rise automatically. You'll still have to defend your resources, of course, but it seems like you won't spend nearly the amount of time making sure that your loser grunts are transporting your goods instead of sitting around... grunting. The streamlined resource plan also means that you won't be defending 30 buildings in town, but more like three or four. You'll still have to use a resource tree, but for instance, instead of building a separate stable yard in order to create knights, a set of stables would add on to the barracks.

 While resources are getting trimmed, units are getting a huge makeover. "We wanted to get away from the fodder effect," says Pardo about the new school of unit building. "We want to try and have less units, and make those units more meaningful to the gamer. Like how every unit has a special ability." Grunts can go berzerk as a special power, whereas a footman can use a defense command that may not be available to other characters, with each unit having a similar sort of special power or skill available only to them. While you'll lose units a lot faster than hero units, the Diablo-skewed character slant to battles now means that you'll care a lot more who dies and who lives in nearly every battle, and you'll also spend a lot more time tweaking individual fighting styles and making battle choices than you would have in the earlier installments. Individuality also translates to the races themselves, with the orcs skilled in melee' combat, the humans given access to a much larger tech tree, and the demons ready to handle a larger range of magic. As for the other races, we'll all have to wait until the company reveals their place in the overall story, as well as their special skills.

 Even the maps themselves are lending a hand to trick you into thinking (a little more than you usually do) that the Warcraft world is a living, breathing environment instead of just a computerized game board. The screens demonstrate the expansive look of the Warcraft world, down to small graphic details such as huts, trees, and waterfalls meant purely to keep your eyes glossy. Pardo also mentions that like Zelda, where one large world was slowly revealed by accomplishing specific tasks in certain areas, Warcraft III wouldn't chop up the world into individual maps, but instead would slowly open up new areas as you progress through missions. The goal is not only to create missions that feel friendly to strategy gamers, but a place where you might spend as much time gaping at the scenery as you would goring.

 And the multiplayer? Pardo says that they've always developed the multiplayer game alongside of the single player adventure, and furthermore, usually develop multiplay first. "We definitely think about multiplayer, how to make it fun," says Pardo about the process. "Once you have a good multiplayer game you can break rules in the single player." It makes sense. Characters may have specific weaknesses and strengths that only come out when fighting with a range of other human players. By discovering that a Knight is especially weak when attacked by a specific combination of units, for instance, then the team will know how to tweak the single player game and the computer AI to make for a more realistic experience. And you can also be fairly certain that by the time this game hits shelves that it will be fully tried, tested, and true in both game modes.

 If Blizzard has its way, Warcraft III will once again change the playing field for strategy/RPG fans everywhere -- and all we can say about it is thank god. Strategy games have begun to smell a bit like that old pond behind the ranch house, but with any luck we'll all be drinking some fresh mountain spring water from Blizzard mountain come this winter. And hopefully I'll stop making wretch-tastic metaphors like the previous one when that time comes.

 -- Vincent Lopez

 September 08, 1999

 Blizzard has a long history of unveiling new titles at the European Computer Trade Show (ECTS), and this year was certainly no exception.  As I'm sure you all know by now, Blizzard's announcement of Warcraft III at ECTS was definitely one of the bigger highlights of the show.  Although the announcement that Blizzard is expanding their top selling fantasy strategy series with another installment came as no surprise, what was rather shocking was the evolution of the game mechanics.

 Warcraft III is set several years after the Beyond the Dark Portal expansion pack and the never-to-be-seen Warcraft Adventures.   The story actually owes a lot to the cancelled adventure game (at least something eventually came of it), following the reunification of the Orcish Horde by the emancipated Orc Thrall.  Thrall was the catalyst in the rejuvenation of the Orc Empire after their fall to the Humans, and eventually helped the warrior race to rediscover the arcane arts wielded by their ancestors.  With the passing of the dominance of the Humans over the Orcs, the two races are once again at war with each other, deciding their differences on the battlefield.  However, now each of the races has a far more sinister foe to deal with ... but more about that later in the preview.

 Although Warcraft III is set in the same universe as the other Warcraft titles, the game has been given a complete facelift and looks very different from its predecessors. Anyone who has played the previous titles will be familiar with the art direction used in the sequel, but Warcraft III will sport an entirely new 3D engine complete with polygonal characters and environments.  It's actually really cool to see the characters from the Warcraft series animated in full 3D. Instead of using a traditional memory-intensive key frame animation system, Blizzard is utilizing a skeletal and skinning data and point animation system which allows for a wider variety of movements than traditional computer animation without consuming a lot of system resources, meaning that you can expect smooth movements from the in-game characters. The landscapes are also looking well detailed at this point, populated with trees, rocky outcroppings, and other accoutrements you would expect in any natural environment.  The more urban settings are shaping up quite nicely as well, complete with shops, wandering NPCs, and raging campfires.

 But the biggest change that Warcraft III fans can expect from this sequel is in the gameplay itself.  Blizzard is calling the third game in the series an RPS (Role-Playing Strategy), not an RTS.  So what does RPS mean?  Well, for starters, instead of focusing on intense resource management and base construction, gameplay in Warcraft III will center more on action, exploration, and questing.  Gone are the days of building placement, carting wood, and demanding micro-management.  The third game in the series will encourage players to interact with a variety of neutral towns instead of relying on just one home base, buying weapons and tools they need for campaigning and recruiting new party members along the way.

 Another key feature borrowed from the role-playing genre will be the movement from a mission-based scheme to a quest-based system.  Gameplay in Warcraft III won't be the typical RTS routine of watching a briefing, completing a mission, viewing a cutscene, and moving on to the next mission.  Instead, players will come across quests while exploring the gameworld which will lead them into new areas for investigation.

 Of course, the main crux of Warcraft III will still center around amassing a group of ruffians and reducing your foes to a bloody pulp.  But gone are the huge armies of the early Warcraft days.  Warcraft III will focus on small adventuring parties comprised of no more than a dozen members led by a powerful hero.  As Producer Rob Pardo described it at the Blizzard press conference, "We're going to have a whole lot of heroes you can control, about six heroes per race, and those heroes will be good enough to actually control your units.  So the heroes are really important because you can't control your units without your heroes being there."  Throughout the game you'll discover more champions to control your troops and, like any RPG game, your heroes will gain experience and advance through levels as you win more battles, becoming more powerful in the process.   Since it is necessary to have a hero to lead your adventuring parties, you may be wondering what happens if one of them dies in the midst of battle?  Well, although it assures that your party will inevitably lose the skirmish, your hero will be transported back to your camp where he can begin the process of recruiting, equipping, and commanding a new squad.

 To add a bit more variety to the series, there will be a total of six races in the game, including the requisite Humans from the armies of Azeroth and the brutish members of the Orcish Horde.  Although Blizzard is keeping tight-lipped about most of the other races, they did reveal the addition of a force of evil Demons known as the Burning Legion into the fray.  One specific Demon unit that Rob told us about at the show was the Infernal, a flame-engulfed beast of malevolence that drops from the sky embedded in a meteor and emerges from the resulting crater.  Now that's an entrance!  Use that one at the next party and just wait for the ladies to flock around you.  The Demons feed off of magical energies and the lifeforce of others, making them a threat to all the races of Azeroth.  This plot twist may perhaps force you to put aside your differences and cooperate with the other races in the game in an attempt to rid the land of these wicked creatures, but this is just speculation at this point as Blizzard wasn't willing to release much hard information on the ongoing story line quite yet.

 You aspiring campaign builders will be delighted to hear that Warcraft III will include a number of extremely powerful game customization tools.  "One of the thing's we've worked on is making sure that people are able to create cool maps, and for Warcraft there's a lot of maps out there," Rob explained. "In Starcraft we started using trigger systems which allowed players to create their own campaigns, and what we're doing this time is we're going even further."  In addition to the simple map editor that most mission designers are accustom to, Warcraft III will expand on the Starcraft trigger-based model to allow for nearly total customization of the game.  You'll be able to write custom scripts for an entire campaign using a Java-like language.  This will allow for ultimate player modification as mission designers will be able to go into each individual trigger and tweak the script however they see fit, in essence allowing you to build an entirely new set of rules with which the game universe operates under.  Blizzard will also be using a non-proprietary file system, making it extremely easy for designers to import custom art and skins into the game.

 While Rob wasn't very specific about the multiplayer options we can expect from Warcraft III (the game is still over a year away, after all), he did hint at deathmatch scenarios as well as co-operative missions and quest-type adventures.  And, as with other Blizzard games, Warcraft III players will be able to meet and compete over Battle.net.

 While the press conference was full of some good hardcore information about the upcoming game, after the announcement on Sunday, I got a chance to sit down with Rob Pardo for a first-hand demonstration of Blizzard's progress so far.  As I mentioned before, it was really exciting to see the Warcraft units animated in full 3D.  The few I saw moved extremely smoothly and I can hardly wait to see more of the models as they're revealed. Rob did show me a small Orcish party comprised of a mounted hero, an Orc grunt (who confidently showed off his skills by juggling his axe behind his back when he wasn't marching), a minotaur, and a wolf rider making their way across a snowy landscape.  While the game engine is fairly complete, the design team still has a long way to go and most of the game content still has to be fleshed out.  Regardless, I did get to see some of the undead units that you'll have to go toe-to-toebone with, and Rob even led the party to a shrine where they freed an old bearded Orc hero.  Another scenario he showed me was how the quest elements will play out in the game.  The Orc party encountered a raised drawbridge which was impassable.  Later in the demo, the group ran across an ancient Orc sage who gave them a clue to lower the bridge and make their way to an unexplored portion of the world.  Unfortunately not all went well for the Orcs as the entire party was frozen by an ice dragon soon after we crossed the bridge.  The demo was short, but at least we got a glimpse of what Blizzard is going for in this third installment of the Warcraft series.

 While Warcraft III is still over a year away, we are extremely excited about the evolution of the Warcraft series, especially since the brief demo looked so impressive at this point.  We can only imagine what the game is going to be like once the development team has a chance to fully flesh out the world.  There has been some debate on the Internet since Blizzard's announcement whether Warcraft III's attempt to redefine the genre has already been done by games such as Heroes of Might and Magic and Warlords.  Instead of calling Warcraft III an RPS, perhaps Blizzard should use the acronym RTRPS (Real Time Role-Playing Strategy) when describing the game.  This would encompass more of the principles behind the game since it still owes a lot to its predecessors, while at the same time attempting to progress the series by leaps and bounds.  However, regardless of its originality, Warcraft III is definitely going to be a big player in next year's line-up, and you can certainly expect to see more about this exciting title throughout its development cycle here at IGNPC.
