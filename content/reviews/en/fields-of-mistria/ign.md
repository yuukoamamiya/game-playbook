# Fields of Mistria Review — ign Review

- Source: [ign](https://www.ign.com/articles/fields-of-mistria-review)
- Author: Jess Reyes
- Published: 2026-08-10
- Score: 9

class="jsx-2805046332 article-content page-0">

I got to a point in Fields of Mistria where I started breeding pink and blue cows, and my new dragon friends are getting acquainted with the townspeople. If that interests you, even without context, then you have a lot to look forward to.

Fields of Mistria is the type of farming sim I wish I had as a kid. It resembles early 2000s cozy classics like Harvest Moon and Animal Crossing, all mixed together into an anime-coded concoction. The world of Mistria brims with deep lore and villagers whose distinct personalities shine through in their dialogue, even after hours of playing. When I wrote my review-in-progress, I was one week into my playthrough – now, over 80 hours later, I can confidently say it’s the best farming sim I’ve played in the last decade.

This fantasy farming-social sim takes place in a pastel pixel art world where mythical creatures and magic exist, but not in a way that overshadows the down-to-earth parts of daily life. I sometimes forget I’m in a magical world when I’m just farming and talking to villagers, and those humble tasks are plenty amusing as is. However, when I walk into the bathhouse and see a giant witch’s cauldron bubbling in the open, it sets me straight with what feels like a whimsical fantasy RPG layer over it. That magical touch grabs my attention, even though most of it still goes to the actual farming loop – similar to Rune Factory, but less overt.

Fields of Mistria  is  about the farming first and foremost, asking you to care for crops, till soil, chop wood, and so on. It’s neat that you can catch fish and insects and donate them to a local museum like in Animal Crossing, and that the cooking and crafting systems give you a meaningful way to use most of the items you find through various recipes. While a lot of this might sound fairly standard, you can also improve your capabilities by giving spiritual currency to your dragon statue, Caldarus, for power-ups like the ability to sometimes get seeds back when you harvest plants or replenish energy when watering.

I saw these activities as an engaging way to speed toward my goals: to grow my farm and revitalize Mistria. However, I couldn’t rush through it all immediately, and I’m glad I couldn’t. I had the urge to start grinding for gold right away, but I was too broke to buy seeds and didn’t have the equipment to clear sticks and rocks from my farm. So, instead of having access to all tools and skills from the beginning, I unlocked them one by one throughout the course of a week in Mistria, and I appreciated how that forced me to slow down and gradually come to grips with its systems, but also really take notice of the townsfolk and the town itself.

 Now, over 80 hours later, I can confidently say it’s the best farming sim I’ve played in the last decade.    “

My average day in Mistria took at least 20 minutes because I talked to every single person I passed by and often paused to flip through menus like the relationship journal that would fill out over time. I spent my first few days completing requests for villagers and ended up getting to know many of them quite well, even ones I wasn’t particularly interested in. These requests, which seemed like simple fetch quests at first, impressed me with the little stories that sometimes came with them. Need some lilacs? Well, you may have just signed up to be a lab experiment for Juniper, the bathhouse owner and resident witch. That quest gave me a glimpse of the shenanigans I could expect from her and her relationship with the children in town.

At the beginning, farming was a bit of a slog because my tiny bag was half filled with tools and I barely had enough money to buy seeds, even after selling my crops and cooked foods. Once you get past this limitation, though, you'll find ways to counter the biggest inconveniences. After about 20 hours, I discovered a purple horse spirit that I could summon at will, so that I could travel faster than walking. Then, the local children started picking up the forageables I left on the ground and leaving them in a box every week. Auntie Elsie started telling me my crushes’ favorite gifts, so I didn’t need to keep guessing what to give them to nurture that relationship.

Every few hours, I would unlock a new way to progress or realize that I’d ignored something that would’ve made my life a lot easier if I’d known it existed hours ago. Teleporting between the mines and my farm was possible after unlocking the stone refinery because of the dragon statue inside, but I hadn’t noticed I could actually teleport using that statue until I tried interacting with it one day by chance.

Fields of Mistria isn't always elegant, however. Some features are missing like the fact you can’t move furniture after placing it and need to use a pickaxe to remove it. Performance-wise, I also don’t love how the game slows to a halt when I open a treasure chest, even after toggling with settings to lessen technical hiccups like that. For the most part, they're minor inconveniences in the grand scheme and easy enough to work around. Many of the customization and accessibility options Field of Mistria offers can potentially make gameplay smoother based on your preferences, like being able to make your days even longer, remove the oversleeping penalty, or turn off specific skills from your dragon statue.

With that in mind, Fields of Mistria is the most fun when I take my time. After spending my mornings on the farm, I may harvest copper from the mines or chop wood until 1:50 am. I might fish for the last entries I’m missing from my museum. And maybe I’ll poke Eiland, my husband and local archeologist, to see new unique dialogue now that we’re married. Sure, some lines are sure to repeat themselves, but the breadth of its writing means I more often see something new and worthwhile.

Some farming sims feel like watching a performance, where the characters only exist in my personal bubble and don’t do anything outside of that – Fields of Mistria subverts this. Seeing characters interact based on a shared history with each other made me feel like an actual newcomer who had a lot to learn about the relationships between townsfolk and the unique quirks that give this town character. I especially enjoyed Friday nights at the Sleeping Dragon Inn, which had every villager gather into one spot for what feels like the whole day; and you see a lot of these dynamics develop in front of you. It’s those moments that convinced me these people actually do talk to each other and have lives when I’m not actively witnessing them.

 The breadth of Mistria's writing means I more often see something new and worthwhile.    “

Even for characters who are based on anime stereotypes (like March, who is unapologetically a tsundere), developer NPC Studio finds a way to uplift them through effective dialogue. Even when I’m mad at March because he insinuated that I’m a leech for accepting a free pickaxe from his brother, I can show up to the bar on Friday and he’s suddenly buddy-buddy with me after a few drinks. Meanwhile, the little girl at the inn told me that she would put Juniper in jail for making stinky potions. Sometimes, I just get a genuine laugh out of them, and it’s certainly praiseworthy when a game like this actually makes me want to read all the dialogue and not just skip it.

Another interesting quirk is that villagers actually react to your presence and the things you do. Someone may go out of their way to tell you where to find a particular item for your fetch quest, or mention seeing a new item in the museum that you just donated. Dialogue can change depending on the time of day, and even more standoffish folks will become more friendly as you get to know them – little quirks like these go a long way in making Mistria more lively.

The only “weak links” are the new Saturday Market vendors, who don’t seem to have as much to say as the original four that visit your town weekly. Taliferro, who hosts cooking challenges, has been asking me about Crystal Berry Pie for weeks, but I'm not exactly compelled to forage in the mines just to shut him up.

Overall, the interactions are so entertaining that I was disappointed I didn’t get more dating-exclusive scenarios or cutscenes where you could witness the more vulnerable parts of your partner before marriage. You track your relationship status based on how many Hearts you've earned with characters, and it maxes out at 10 total. Eight hearts is when you can either start dating or become “best friends,” so heart events skip straight from scenarios that could be read as platonic to engagement eligible events at 10 hearts. Fields of Mistria triggers a new event for each bachelor and bachelorette every two hearts until you reach 8 for friendship and 10 when dating, at which point you can ask to talk about marriage.

It also implements a dating system where you can ask bachelors and bachelorettes on dates after agreeing to romantically pursue them. On Saturday and Sunday, you could pick one person to go on a date with and earn extra heart points from it. However, these dates are completely dialogue-free and that feels like a missed opportunity when dating has historically been integral to farming sims.

In that process, I wish that dating events like the Shooting Star Festival had separate scenarios for characters at different heart levels. A few characters are harder to get to know, and they're the ones who tend to be more aloof even when watching the stars together. It would’ve been cool to see how the interactions change after you get closer, too. For example, I wanted to see how March would act during the festival once I actually started dating him, but it was a completely text-free interaction.

Fields of Mistria lets you date as many people as you want without negative consequences and actively encourages you to get to know bachelors and bachelorettes. It doesn’t shame you for feeling interested in more than one person, and I loved that as someone who wants to experience multiple heart events on one account out of pure curiosity. You can only marry one person, anyway. After getting engaged, the rest of your dating pool breaks up with you, but you can turn that off in the settings if you want. Either way, it’s no hard feelings.

When you do choose a partner, Fields of Mistria adds flair to the marriage by letting you pick your own wedding date, discuss with your fiancee about what flowers and food to have at the venue, and see reactions from others about the engagement. It really makes your wedding a special day and not just an objective to check off a list.

Little things about marriage are also cute, like the fact that your spouse will wander around your home and do mundane things like use the kitchen, sit on the couch to read a book, or sift through shelves. They may even comment about your magical cat or dog, or stop what they’re doing to go to bed with you if you decide to end the day with them in the same room.

 I can only look forward to NPC Studio’s future additions, but as it stands, Mistria already has my heart.    “

Tonally, Fields of Mistria leans much more into lighthearted antics. It touches on the insecurities of its characters, but doesn’t dig deeply into the ugly parts of human life as conflicts between characters are easily resolved. While some personalities are more one-note than others, the way characters react to each other and the world around them make them seem more well-rounded without portraying much angst. Mistria is a wholesome town where we all get to be friends, and that’s perfectly fine.

There are two main stories that drive Fields of Mistria: the Town Repair Questline and Mines Questline. The Town Repair Questline is the main reason you’re even in Mistria, since you’re answering the town’s call for aid from the Adventurer’s Guild. Following this questline gradually unlocks new features like bigger inventory from the General Store, a mill for processing animal products, and more vendors for the Saturday Market. But the Mines Questline in particular motivated me to dig all the way to the bottom floor of the dungeon it revolves around because of the mystery behind the “shrines” unveiled every 10 to 20 floors and for how it integrated specific characters like Eiland and Juniper into its story.

The mines themselves are where you bring out your inner adventurer – Fields of Mistria is still an RPG after all, you'll be fighting monsters in the mines. Your combat kit is limited to swords, a fire spell, and buffs you can unlock from dragon statues. But considering how the mines are mostly a means to get ores for upgrading farming tools, it didn’t need to be an in-depth endeavor. None of the enemies were particularly challenging, and the monster variations are distinct enough from each other to stave off repetition.

After 80 hours, I'm still compelled to keep playing Fields of Mistria. There's more to do in the Town Repair Questline because building Renown, the XP for your town, is a slow burn when I’m casually completing quests, donating to the museum, and shipping items. And perhaps I'll have a child one of these days, but I don’t feel like I need to sprint to get there.

I’m in what I’d consider the “endgame” and, even if I were to stop here, I’d feel like Fields of Mistria would have been well worth my time. At the beginning of my third year, I can still see myself having enough to enjoy another full year, especially since the Friday Nights at the Inn have started including new characters I unlocked by completing the Mines Questline – and it's those moments that make the experience feel special rather than routine.

### Verdict

Fields of Mistria is the most fun I’ve had playing a farming sim since Rune Factory 4, which was the last time I remember villagers having as detailed personal lives as they do in Mistria. The level of care that NPC Studio has put into its characters and the moments that make Mistria feel like a lived-in town is vast, and I was consistently surprised throughout my 80-plus hours with how they made everyday life and its interactions undeniably charming. Minor shortcomings like interactions in the relationship process not being as fleshed out don't overshadow what it does so well. It also gives you the reins to min-max your farm, decorate, and collect to your heart’s content with few restrictions, and makes sure you don't have that nagging feeling like you're falling behind if you decide to focus on one thing at a time. I can only look forward to NPC Studio’s future additions, but as it stands, Mistria already has my heart.
