# Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave review - a strategy RPG great — eurogamer Review

- Source: [eurogamer](https://www.eurogamer.net/fire-emblem-fortunes-weave-review)
- Author: Dom Peppiatt
- Published: 2026-09-16
- Score: 5

A synergy of everything that has made the series great for generations, Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave hits the pinnacle of combat design, and is a sumptuous feast of a game.

I cannot remember the last time I put this many hours into a game for a review. And still, I'm sitting here, writing this, itching to play more of Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave. Despite some odd decisions for  an RPG of this depth and scale  - a single save slot and diluted difficulty, to name two - I haven't played a game that has captivated and fascinated me like this in years. This is Fire Emblem at its peak, a game as sharp and satisfying as  Awakening  on the 3DS, with character work and story as compelling as  Three Houses . It summons the same sense of wonder and scale as those multi-disc PS1 RPGs did back in the 90s. Developer Intelligent Systems has refined all the map-building, scenario-setting work it did with  Engage  and iterated it alongside the storytelling tour de force of Three Houses to create something exceptional, no matter what direction you look at it from. This, right here, is a reason to buy a  Switch 2 .

## Fire Emblem: Fortune's Weave

-  Developer:  Intelligent Systems

-  Publisher:  Nintendo

-  Platform:  Played on Switch 2

-  Availability:  Out 17th September on  Switch 2

Let's start at the end of the world, shall we? An introductory section of the game shows you the end of everything. It's up to you, mysterious God-like avatar, to rewind time to the Heroic Games of 1449 and peer into the political intrigue of the Dagdan continent to unravel how we got to this undead-filled apocalypse. It's all very anime, very high stakes and high drama, and honestly, this introductory crawl is the worst bit of the game. But, if you can stick with the megaton exposition dump and endure the slow-paced tutorial battles, you'll crack open the oddly sci-fi-flavoured carapace to feast on the rich nectar of fantasy role-playing within.

With the narrative wrapper discarded, you get to see what the game is really about: four distinct paths, following four distinct characters. You're limited to one save slot, because all the paths are intrinsically linked: you can hop between stories at will (though I'd recommend at least giving yourself  some  guardrails to ensure progression, otherwise you'll never make it out of Chapter 1). By 80 hours and counting, for what it's worth, I had played each of the four paths - though two were somewhat rushed for timing's sake and I played only story-critical missions for those. I reckon a full, leisurely playthrough of this game will take a good 200 hours, if you're a completionist. But even that may be conservative.

You've probably seen our core cast  in the trailers : the four main protagonists are Theodora (the loyal, citizen-first warrior queen), Dietrech (a violent and intelligent foreigner determined to uncover Dagda's mysteries), Cai (a young village boy caught up in a peculiar conspiracy), and Leda (a traveling performer lusting for revenge). The quality, depth, and voice acting work across all four stories is great, though I have to single out Leda's path as the most emotive and well-acted of the lot. You've got more production value in just one of these paths than most smaller RPGs get in their entire runtime, honestly. You just have to accept that the majority of plot-first cutscenes are going to happen in one of the same 30-or-so backdrops, with very minimal over-the-shoulder camerawork. It's not really a problem though, because the writing is generally good enough to hold your attention, and the little animated portraits do more than enough to convey what's going on.

As you pluck away at the knotted, labyrinthine plot, you start to become aware of the impossibly complex web Intelligent Systems has spun here. I kept expecting to run into the same fights on the same maps in different paths, but Intelligent Systems has somehow placed and paced everything so that  every single path  feels different. Even when you do team up Theodora and Cai, for example, the convoluted route to get there is so different - and the final battle so twisted - that you wouldn't even really know they were the same fight without the prompts. There's probably twice as much going on here as Three Houses, and that's an ungenerous take.

It's better, too: whilst Three Houses excelled in some ways, there was a little bloat either side of the (in)famous timeskip that hampered the overall game a little - the school sections sometimes felt prolonged, and the pacing sometimes a little clumsy. Fortune's Weave has been engineered to cater more to your tastes; if you don't like the 'social simulation' side of nu-Fire Emblem, you don't have to engage with it. If you're more interested in giving the right gifts to the right people, and filling your army with just about every bonus unit imaginable, you'll need to create a spreadsheet to min-max it properly. But Intelligent Systems doesn't  need  you to play either way: There's more agency - and urgency, at times - in Fortune's Weave than Three Houses ever managed. I think there's a three-way split here: the opening hours of the game appeal to nu-spec Fire Emblem fans with its Persona-flavoured social focus, and Chapter 2 is more akin to the itinerant fights of 2005's Radiant Dawn or 2008's Shadow Dragon. The last portion stands alone. I won't divulge too much of that here for both spoiler and embargo reasons.
