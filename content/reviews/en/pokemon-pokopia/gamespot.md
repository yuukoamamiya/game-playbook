I audibly gasped. My small community of Pokemon friends had been steadily growing in the first hour or so of Pokemon Pokopia, and now Professor Tangrowth asked me to build a house and mark it as my own. I could even invite another Pokemon to live there with me, he said. You mean I can have a Squirtle roommate?! I built my new house as quickly as I could and invited Squirtle to come live in it, and he happily agreed. Childhood dream fulfilled.

A few days later, Squirtle told me he wanted to move out.

Heartbroken and with nothing to do to change Squirtle’s mind, I pressed on. I was determined to learn more, earn more, and do more, so that, one day, Squirtle will come back. Someday I’ll make a new house and I’ll invite Squirtle to live with me again. Maybe he just wanted his own bedroom? Admittedly, the leaf hut is not a lot of space. Maybe he didn’t like my decor at the time–a few items I had arranged slapdash to fulfill the housing requirements.

Now obviously, this is a video game, and all of the Pokemon are running on relatively simple routines. But Pokopia potently delivers on the fantasy of creating a community of Pokemon friends, meeting them as equals, and trying to understand their unique interests. And it does all of this in an incredibly flexible world full of secrets to discover and pieces to break apart and put back together as you wish. All of this combines to make it one of the best Pokemon spin-offs ever, and an excellent way to celebrate the series’ 30th anniversary.

We’ve been trained, so to speak, to look at Pokemon the way that humans in the real world look at animals. As a starting point, Pokemon Pokopia pulls a Pokemon Mystery Dungeon and breaks this mold by placing you in the floppy shoes of a Pokemon: the shapeshifting Ditto. You take the rough shape of a human–your human, your trainer, who has gone missing–but you are, through and through, a Ditto. You relate to other Pokemon as equals, they casually call you “Ditto” as a nickname, and you speak their language. Your ability to change shape and look human gives you the ability to help them out in ways they can’t help themselves, but you are not their trainer.

Your human isn’t the only one who has gone missing. Pokopia envisions a post-human world, continuing the pattern of the spin-off games typically approaching a darker subject matter than the mainline titles ever do. Everywhere you look, there are signs that human society has broken down and nature is reclaiming it. You’ll occasionally find old records of what the humans were up to just before they disappeared. Where they all went and how to get them back is the central driving mystery behind the game.

This all probably sounds like a downer, but playing it is anything but. The moment-to-moment gameplay of Pokemon Pokopia is joyful and breezy. It’s cute, watching this little society of Pokemon try to rebuild based on their best guesses and vague memories of how humans lived.

As a gaming touchpoint, Pokemon Pokopia most strongly resembles the Dragon Quest Builders series. The world is constructed of building cubes, almost all of which can eventually be destroyed and replaced with whatever you want. As a Ditto, you have the unique ability to learn the special moves of the Pokemon you find, which allows you to pick up all sorts of skills required for tilling soil, planting tall grass, cutting down weeds, breaking through boulders, swimming across the sea, and even gliding. Professor Tangrowth gives you mission-critical tasks to advance the story, but you could spend as long as you want beautifying your town and optimizing the environment for your Pokemon friends.

Discovering new Pokemon is one of the best parts of Pokopia, and a total paradigm shift from the usual Pokemon formula. You’re no longer using other Pokemon to battle the creatures you find, hoping to wear them out and then tossing a Poke Ball to capture them. Instead, you’re building habitats to attract new species. These are presented as simple environmental puzzles, and a new “Habitat Dex” helps you keep track of them all.

Four tufts of tall grass joined in any orientation could attract any of the classic Kanto starters–Squirtle, Bulbasaur, or Charmander–along with some others. But those same four tufts planted next to a tree will attract a Scyther or Bellsprout. A small field of flowers will bring a Pidgey and Combee, while a much larger field of flowers will attract the attention of a Vespiqueen. Then there are habitats that require specific objects or props, like a punching bag next to a bench to capture the attention of a Hitmonchan. There are just scads of material and object types to use, and each one tells a little feng shui story about the type of Pokemon you’re attracting.

Drawing a wide variety of Pokemon, or Pokemon with certain skills, is often required for advancing story missions. You can find habitat hints laying around the ground, but if you’re stuck, you can always purchase new hints at a shop as well. Discovering hints is always exciting, because you get to see a silhouette of a Pokemon you could attract using it, and their iconic designs make them easy to read for longtime fans. I would find a hint and immediately think to myself, “Oh, I can make that!” and start right away, or rush back to my base camp to pick up some necessary materials from storage. It’s a satisfying experience similar to building a Lego set–you have the instructions and it’s up to you to find the pieces and snap it together, and then your reward is a new little friend.

You earn currency by completing objectives like planting crops or meeting new Pokemon, so you’re rewarded for the things you’re already doing anyway, and that feeds back into the loop of letting you do even more with your money. Each area culminates in some larger objective that requires the cooperation of several Pokemon, after which point, a gate opens to a new location and the cycle repeats. But you can always tell your friends to tag along between areas, and revisit old ones and gather materials.

The system is flexible because, among other things, lots of Pokemon have the same sets of specialties. Bulbasaur has the Grow ability that gives your crops and flowers a burst of growth, but if your resident Bulbasaur isn’t around, you could also ask an Oddish. Occasionally, you’ll also meet a story-critical named Pokemon, like Mosslax, a Snorlax who snoozed so long that moss grew all over his body; or Peakychu, a Pikachu who burned out his electricity-generating abilities and looks a bit droopy and sickly. These fun and cute twists on classic Pokemon designs are always a delight and help give the game even more personality.

The sheer variety of Pokemon on display is impressive. My Pokedex is already several dozen entries long and I haven’t even discovered all of those, and there are surely more to attract that I haven’t found hints for yet. The roster leans heavily on classic Pokemon, like Eevee, alongside more recent ones that share similarly simplistic designs, like Dartrix. That appears to be a conscious choice to lean into the art style, which is bright and bubbly, and looks great when rendering one of those classically styled Pokemon. When it gets into more complex design territory with legendary Pokemon, the art style falters a little and looks sillier. That said, the 30 years of Pokemon designs means Pokopia has plenty to curate from across the spectrum of games. I’ve seen Pokemon from Red and Blue all the way to the Scarlet and Violet generations, and there are many more to uncover.

The vast majority of Pokemon are presented as happy, sweet little friends, but Pokopia does manage to imbue some personality in surprising ways, and the writing is frequently funny. It’s more chuckle-inducing than gut-busting, but funny nonetheless. I enjoyed hearing Drifloon idly comment on you floating away with her, or Machoke talk like a gym bro. Other times the writing is warm and sweet, especially with the named characters, who are grappling with the loss of the humans. Peakychu’s backstory, in particular, made him very endearing.

Sometimes you’ll just capture little slices of Pokemon life. Once, I was alerted to a photo op and got out my camera to snap a picture of Charmander sleeping by the campfire. As I lined up my shot, I saw that Squirtle was peacefully sniffing flowers in the background. This wasn’t a second photo op, just a little moment captured on camera that I happened to see, and that made the world feel that much more alive.

Similarly, the world itself is bursting with secrets to uncover. More than once, I wandered in a direction only to discover something amazing. I ventured underground into an active volcano and discovered a massive castle. Another time, I found what appeared to be underground bunkers complete with tons of old human decorations and artifacts. I was more than 15 hours in before I idly wondered what was behind a breakable wall in the very first locale, only to find a vast secret area. There are layers upon layers upon layers, both figuratively and literally, to explore.

And like Animal Crossing, Pokopia uses a real-time clock. For the most part, this doesn’t come into play, other than creating a brief pause when starting the game from Sleep Mode while it catches up on what time of day you’re playing. Sometimes you’ll need to wait a requisite amount of time for construction to finish or for plants to grow, but these are minor inconveniences. I suspect they impacted me more as a reviewer on a deadline than a regular player. All in all, these minor moments of timed friction seem aimed to slow you down and encourage you to do other things. You don’t always need to rush to complete the next story objective. Sometimes you can just explore and build and take your time.

Pokemon Pokopia is a combination of the design sensibilities of Animal Crossing and Dragon Quest Builders, but by melding those two structures and fusing them with Pokemon, it compensates for each one’s weaknesses. Animal Crossing is so focused on building community that there isn’t much to do once you finish your daily chores, while Dragon Quest Builders relies heavily on story quests without much incentive to build up your community and socialize. Pokemon Pokopia gives you a massive amount to do and a story that propels you forward, while also letting you enjoy the simple pleasure of living among your Pokemon friends and building your perfect community. I feel like I’ve barely scratched the surface, and I can’t wait to keep exploring. I’ll get Squirtle back yet.
