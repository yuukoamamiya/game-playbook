# Resident Evil Requiem — GameSpot Review

- Source: [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/resident-evil-requiem-review-two-headed-mutant/1900-6418464/)
- Author: Phil Hornshaw
- Published: February 23, 2026
- GameSpot score: 8 / 10 — Great

## Resident Evil Requiem Review – Two-Headed Mutant

With Requiem, Capcom refines its Resident Evil formula to its best parts, but it sometimes relies too much on past triumphs.

The Resident Evil series has a long history of struggling to find the right balance of horror and action, sometimes becoming massively successful and influential in either genre, and sometimes completely faceplanting after leaning too far one way. Resident Evil Requiem, the ninth mainline game in the series, sees Capcom dialing in the combination of those elements better than ever, though in a somewhat inelegant way. Rather than try to blend different elements of two different genres into a single experience, it just staples together two distinct experiences that each capture the best parts of Resident Evil–to the point where it is almost two separate games running in parallel.

One game is a slow, frightening, gory haunted house story following an everyday person as its protagonist, hewing close to the horror-first approach of Resident Evil 7: Biohazard. The other is a fast-paced, panic-inducing experience starring an action-hero badass that draws directly from Resident Evil 4. Requiem even lets you set different points of view for the separate protagonists, recommending RE7's first-person approach for horror and RE4's third-person camera for action, though you can use either for both.

Disparate as they may be, though, both halves are extremely compelling. Requiem feels like Resident Evil's developers, for the most part, recognizing what they do well and leaning in all the way. The result is a game that's unwilling to leave the track set by its predecessors, but one that still provides an intense, often exciting ride.

The haunted house half of Requiem focuses on Grace Ashcroft, an everywoman FBI analyst. Grace is exceedingly ordinary and relatable; while she has some training and can handle a weapon, she's not physically imposing, and so mostly has to rely on moving carefully and quietly to survive. She's not used to dealing with the horde of monsters she'll come to face through the course of the game, and actress Angela Sant'Albanolines delivers all her lines with a believably quaking, halting terror.

Grace's portion draws directly from the classic Resident Evil formula. She quickly finds herself kidnapped by a scary individual and trapped in a hospital that's also a secret research facility for viruses that turn people into zombies. You're forced to wander the place with limited weapons and ammo, searching for supplies to keep yourself alive and keys to open new areas, while the former staff and patients try to make a meal out of you.

Zombies can still be pretty scary, and Requiem takes them a step further than previous games, with this batch of T-virus-mutated undead maintaining a bit of their memories. You'll find a zombie maid trying to clean the bathroom, a zombie chef cleaving through piles of indiscriminate meat in the kitchen, or a zombie singer belting out a few notes in the lounge. Requiem's zombies can carry weapons and even kind of use them, though with diminished motor control, making them more dangerous and unpredictable when they attack.

Their little habits and bits of characterization can also be exploited, putting them into patterns that you can use to slip past them or even leverage against other zombies, which makes them much more interesting than the undead we've seen before. For instance, in one room, two zombies shuffled around with their backs to me, and one kept slurring through a complaint about noise. When I bashed through a nearby wooden box to grab an item, the crashing sound so enraged the complainer that he ripped apart his pal, while giving me a chance to escape.

That little bit of additional, ahem, *life* in the zombies makes them compelling enemies, which is good, because they're the main threat you'll face. There are a few other creatures that pop up here and there, but Requiem's bestiary is surprisingly thin. Zombies do offer some extra variety though, occasionally mutating into "blisterheads" that are much faster, more aggressive, and tougher to kill. The bodies of zombies you drop with gunfire or knife wounds stay on the ground, and sometimes, those seemingly dead monsters will resurrect as much deadlier blisterheads.

This adds quite a bit to the overall dread of playing as Grace. Running back and forth through the same halls of the hospital, you'll pass the bodies of zombies you've already defeated and wonder every single time whether they'll start convulsing and screaming before rising to their feet. Knowing that any zombie you kill could come back even worse adds another thing to worry about every time you pull the trigger, alongside the dwindling number of bullets in your pocket and the fear that more zombies will hear your gun and come shambling.

The addition of blisterheads isn't anything new–they're almost exactly like the crimson head variant of zombies from the 2002 remake of the original Resident Evil, and enemies that get worse and pop back up for a second round have been a mainstay in some form or another ever since. But it's still a damn good trick, and like a lot of other ideas in Requiem, it's honed to a fine edge–especially when it's paired with the game's crafting system.

After a while in the hospital, Grace discovers the ability to craft items, including ammo for her guns, using infected blood. Once you have the means of gathering blood, you can draw it from the puddles and buckets of gore scattered throughout the game, turning previous horror set-dressing into a gameplay mechanic. You can also extract it from some dead enemies, which is a great touch; just as you start learning about the possibility of blisterheads, you find a reason to get *very* close to corpses.

The best thing you can craft with infected blood is a "hemolytic injector," a one-use syringe that can destroy any zombie, causing it to literally explode in a shower of blood and guts. An injector can even be used on a zombie corpse to eliminate it before it can become a blisterhead. The infected blood crafting mechanic makes zero sense in reality, but it adds an interesting wrinkle to resource management, while also giving you all the more incentive to be calculating. If you can sneak up on a zombie, you can inject and destroy it outright, saving ammo.

But infected blood and the materials you need to craft useful items are limited. The same stuff needed to make a hemolytic injector can make handgun bullets, or an ink ribbon needed to save your game on certain difficulties, or even an upgrade for Grace to make her more resilient. Every single item you make means there are several others you can't, and coupled with managing your limited inventory space, the crafting system heightens the anxiety inherent in trying to guess what tools you're going to need.

Grace doesn't have the resources, the training, or the strength to fight everything she encounters, and that's especially true for a few hulking enemies that stalk her through the hospital and that she can only evade. Dealing with these enemies offers excellent moments that create a ton of tension, giving you some contextual tools like keys and doors that you'll find yourself sprinting to and activating *just in time*. Again, these ideas were essential to Resident Evil 7 and Village–and Requiem uses them to great effect, too, with level design that gives you a little more agency than just running away, but still maintains high anxiety.

But Resident Evil games are also about action, and occasionally battling mutated foes with grenade launchers and shotguns. The role of action-hero protagonist this evening is performed by Leon S. Kennedy, returning in *most of* his Resident Evil 4 glory.

Leon is running an investigation parallel to Grace's, so they wind up on intersecting trajectories throughout the story and sometimes following each other through the same locations, with the enemies you killed and resources you grabbed as one character affecting what's left for the other. In the first half of the game, Leon's appearances are sparse, mostly being 15-minute cameos whenever Requiem needs a boss fight. This makes for a haphazard structure that can be jarring, but which effectively breaks up the gameplay to relieve one kind of stress by providing another.

The differences between the ways that Leon and Grace play are drastic, with Leon's sections emulating Resident Evil 4's panicky desperation as you scramble around tight spaces, slowly running out of resources and finding yourself cornered by monsters who shake off most bullets.

At its base, most of the challenges Leon faces aren't particularly different from those posed by RE4's Los Illuminados enemies. Where Requiem builds on that formula is in increasing the chaos of any given situation. In the first room you enter in the hospital, Leon is suddenly accosted by zombie patients and doctors, one of whom busts into the room carrying a chainsaw. Shoot that guy, and the chainsaw clatters to the ground, spinning around dangerously and threatening to slice your ankles–until another zombie grabs it and hauls it into the air, with their lack of control sending the blade careening through the limbs of their infected cohorts. These kinds of moments make battles all the more ridiculous and unpredictable, forcing you to change the way you move and fight as zombie stupidity turns to serendipity–or catastrophe.

You can grab some of those dropped weapons yourself, spearing a zombie in the chest with an IV rack or tossing a propane tank into a crowd to set a horde on fire. And when that chainsaw stops spinning, that can be yours too. Monsters have been chasing Leon with chainsaws for two decades, and it is *incredibly* satisfying to pick one up yourself in response.

Leon carries a dedicated melee weapon of his own, a hatchet, which gives you fresh options for how you deal with monsters. Slashing away with the hatchet can conserve ammo, but the weapon is best when applied to finish enemies quickly. Once a zombie is down or hurt enough, you can get in close and dispatch it with the hatchet, making sure it won't get back up as a blisterhead. The hatchet is a fun addition that rewards developing your fighting skills, but the blade dulls fast, forcing you to stop and sharpen it just like you have to reload your guns.

The hatchet also provides Leon with a parry ability he can use to deflect just about any attack. The parry is the hatchet's real value, and it adds a dimension to the panicky gameplay that rewards you for your twitch reflexes. Especially in later boss fights, the parry gives you a means of dealing with attacks that feel bad to try to dodge, given Requiem's tight camera and relatively slow-moving player characters. It's a small but deliberate improvement to RE4's take on combat, making Leon feel like a highly effective soldier while still keeping him a bit clunky to maintain the overall tension. Leon can handle one attacker, but several are still going to give him big problems.

Leon gets a bigger spotlight in the second half Requiem, which also features the much-advertised return to Raccoon City. For players with a particular history with Resident Evil's classic setting, it's a fascinating return, especially once the game starts sending you to important locations like the Raccoon City Police Department. The return works particularly well thanks to Capcom's recent remakes of its classic RE games. This RPD, partially destroyed by the missile strike that annihilated much of the city, is otherwise a perfect match to the one we fought through in 2019's Resident Evil 2 remake, making it just the right amount of familiar. The moment also offers some genuinely compelling characterization for Leon, a man who is usually little more than a series of quips piled inside a leather jacket–albeit only a bit.

Heading back to Raccoon City is thrilling, especially for a player who has been plugged into this franchise since the beginning, but it can also all feel a little *too* familiar and a bit *too* interested in the past. That's a systemic problem for Requiem, to some degree. Resident Evil is an aging band playing all its hits–and *only* its hits. But it did that with Village, too. And with remake after remake. Venturing through a destroyed Raccoon City is captivating, but the deeper into the city you go, the more you find that there's just not much there, and Requiem at times risks retreading too much ground.

In some cases, Requiem has cut away so much of the fat from Resident Evil's ideas that it has left them thin and wasting. Most of Requiem's puzzles aren't really puzzles at all, and it's rare that you won't know exactly what you need to do with an object or a key almost immediately upon discovering it. For example, when I found an organ donation cooler with an intricate lock hidden on the back, I was intrigued, until about a half-hour later when I found a piece of paper with a diagram showing exactly how to arrange the myriad dials and switches to open it. The puzzles feel like their main purpose isn't to test your brainpower, but to give you something to do other than sneaking, shooting, and worrying, and they offer little to make them satisfying.

There were a few points where I found Requiem pretty frustrating, mostly in moments when the game turned from the underlying formula without providing much in the way of explanation of what it wanted from me, and the only way to figure it out was to die a few times. A portion in the destroyed Raccoon City sees zombified soldiers perfunctorily operating mortar launchers as you try to find and eliminate them, but between the mortar shells that are tough to see coming and the assault rifle-wielding zombies spraying bullets that are extremely tough to avoid, I got blasted a few too many times before I finally figured out how I was meant to approach the situation.

The same was true of an otherwise-excellent sequence that upended the usual approach to fighting zombies in favor of using the environment as a weapon. Despite beating all the enemies in this portion very effectively, I kept dying when the whole area would suddenly collapse underneath me. It was only after several tries that I discovered why–in using the environment against the zombies, I was cutting myself off from the area's exit, so the game would kill me and reset the encounter. But Requiem had neglected to mention that I should be trying to reach this particular spot in the first place, so I had no idea I needed to exercise caution in how I was changing things. Some people like to complain about games using gobs of yellow paint to mark every interactive element, but moments like unexpectedly dying over and over until something clicks are why developers opt for making those things so obvious.

When Requiem is on, though–especially during Grace's portions–it is *very* on. And while the horror and action elements are separated, they never feel at odds; both experiences are tense and exciting in their specific ways. Requiem feels like two different games stapled together, but they're two very good games, and they complement one another, allowing each to do what it's best at and to forgo many of the things it wouldn't handle as well.

For almost a decade, Capcom has been refining Resident Evil, finding ways to modernize the storied franchise and to maintain what has made it so beloved. With Requiem, Capcom has dialed in Resident Evil maybe as far as it can. The result is a game that leans too hard on past successes and nostalgia, and so doesn't show its fans any new ideas. But it knows its hits backward and forward, and it plays them near-perfectly.

### Review Verdict

**Score: 8 / 10 — Great**

**The Good**

- Zombies are excellent, with human-like tendencies that makes each one interesting
- Offers some of the best of Resident Evil's horror- and action-focused games
- Additions to the formula, like Grace's blood crafting and Leon's parry, are small but meaningful
- Two different approaches offer a variety in terms of tension and stress, and both are great
- Returning to Raccoon City is fascinating and nostalgic

**The Bad**

- Some elements, like puzzles, have been too heavily simplified or thinned out
- Portions where the only way to figure out what to do is to die a few times get frustrating
- So reliant on nostalgia and past successes that it can feel like a retread
