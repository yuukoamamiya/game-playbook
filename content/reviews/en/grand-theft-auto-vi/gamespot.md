# Grand Theft Auto 6 Sets The Bar For Open Worlds Impossibly High — GameSpot Review

- Source: [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/grand-theft-auto-6-review/)
- Author: Tamoor Hussain
- Published: 2026-11-18
- GameSpot score: 10/10

A few hours into Grand Theft Auto 6, I encountered a fly.

Lucia, my partner in life, love, and crime, was set to be released from prison, so, as Jason, I got to work repairing my ramshackle home. I placed drywall, fixed stairs, painted rooms, and rearranged furniture. After a hard day’s work, I headed to the kitchen to grab a drink, and that’s when I heard it: a passing buzz uncomfortably close to my ears. There was a fly in the kitchen. I spun my camera around to track it down, but I never did. In fact, I didn’t see it or hear it ever again.

In the 60-plus hours of illicit drug deals, impromptu jewelry-store robberies, daring supercar thefts, and high-stakes bank heists, I kept being reminded of that moment with the fly. Among all the people you can interact with and amidst all the things you can do in Leonida, there is a fly that players _might_ hear and _probably_ won’t see. And someone put it there.

This is, to me, the best illustration of what GTA 6 is: a game of staggering scope and scale obsessed with small details that could be lost in its vastness, a giant haystack full of needles that you’ll constantly find, creating delight in their improbable discovery.

While there are numerous immersive activities to spend your time on outside the boundaries of the Bonnie-and-Clyde-like story at its center, your experience may not be much like mine when you explore. But it’s that variety and depth, matched with an unrivaled cinematic flair and thrilling gameplay, that leaves GTA 6 in a league of its own. And, somehow, the execution of it all is as close to flawless as I’ve ever seen.

I say “somehow,” but the method is clear: perfection achieved through repetition and iteration. I think it’s fair to say that GTA 6 is, in many ways, the same game that Rockstar has been making since GTA 3. It shares the same structural framework and gameplay ideas as that game and every entry that followed.

Like in most other GTA titles, one of the first missions you’ll do introduces Pay ‘n’ Spray, the auto shop where you can repaint vehicles to reduce how aggressively the cops are pursuing you. It’s a familiar mainstay mission among many others whose broad strokes are some combination of: driving somewhere; picking and/or dropping something and/or someone off; and getting into chases and/or gunfights on land, sea, or in air.

What differentiates each GTA game from one another and prevents a feeling of staleness in the series is the world around you in each one, how you interact with it, and how it reacts to you. What makes GTA 6’s Leonida distinct is the feeling of independence it instills. The appeal of GTA, and the fantasy it facilitates, has traditionally been in creating chaos to agitate a world that reacts to your every move–be it through police trying to apprehend you with escalating levels of force, or random people fighting or fleeing in response to your behavior. It’s an experience that is centered around your character and you as the player, so, naturally, all the systems the series’ cities have operated on have had their focus trained on you.

But that has changed over time to reflect GTA stories in which no single character really is the _main_ character, and GTA 6 is the first game in the long-running series that feels truly detached from and ambivalent to your presence. It exists and functions with or without you. The depth and breadth of Leonida, GTA 6’s Miami simulacrum, is such that the city is always embroiled in some form of Florida-flavored chaos, irrespective of you. In fact, _you_ are often the one who needs to make the decision of when, where, and how to react to _it_.

Chaos is relative, but it comes in all shapes, sizes, and levels of extreme in GTA 6. Early in the game, I was driving and heard a man pleading for mercy, so I clicked the DualSense’s thumbsticks to slow things down and look for visualized waves of sound, utilizing a smart way of drawing attention to something noteworthy that Rockstar introduced in Red Dead Redemption 2. I approached the disturbance to find a man being robbed at gunpoint by an elderly lady on a mobility scooter. As a fan of both people-watching and rubbernecking, I decided to see how the situation played out.

The man handed over his wallet, and the lady on the scooter began to make her getaway. But then she spotted me and demanded I also hand over my valuables. As in Red Dead Redemption 2, I was able to focus on her and select from a few responses, so I told her that she should rethink the second phase of this crime. She didn’t.

I responded by pulling out my gun and firing at the ground next to her. She freaked out and made an escape so slow that I was able to walk beside her at a leisurely pace as she screamed for help. Mobility scooters are not great getaway vehicles, it turns out. Eventually, I remembered she dropped the wallet and went to return it. But, given I just pulled a gun on an old-age pensioner, the original robbery victim saw me coming and fled in terror. I was left standing there with someone else’s money–and the cops looking for me, rather than a scooter-bound mugger on the run for granny theft auto.

This kind of emergent moment is a frequent occurrence: a cop accidentally tasing his partner as he attempts to apprehend a lady trying to steal his car in a traffic stop gone wrong; a man that pops up out of a dumpster to announce he is now my lawyer; a plane transporting drugs crashing near me; a drunk driver acting erratically on purpose to catch a cop off-guard and make a break for freedom.

Ordinarily, I would avoid talking about these moments so as not to spoil them, but this all happened within the first few hours of the game. There are a baffling number of events like these throughout, and I know that I only experienced a small subset of them. They are just one small but thoughtfully placed gear in a large and very complicated machine that keeps Leonida feeling dynamic and brimming with surprises.

Spending ample time with your partner is worth the effort

It’s made even more effective by how convincingly ordinary the underpinnings of the city are. There are fully functional bus and train systems that you can use to get around the city; just interact with a bus stop and it’ll tell you when the next one is arriving. You can hop on, put your headphones on and listen to the radio, or maybe one of the true-crime podcasts on your phone. While you’re doing that, you can check your texts, read your reminders to see who you need to respond to, use the BuckMe app to manage your finances, and hop on SnapMatic to doomscroll, like pictures, snoop on profiles, and even check out locations in the city that might pique your interest based on an image. Or you can just fast-track the entire journey.

I used the bus only two or three times in my entire playthrough and didn’t use the trains at all. And if you get tired of walking and don’t want to steal a car, there’s a version of Lyft you can use where each driver has their own personality; some want to chat, others not so much. Each is another kind of fly in my kitchen: an elaborate system that I often ignored, but appreciated for how much it added to immersion.

The way driving works is similarly authentic. There are traffic lights, road signs, lanes, and all the other rules of the road that, for the most part, people try to follow. And when Leonida’s citizens encounter rule-breakers, they react appropriately. I can’t count the number of times I sped up and changed lanes a little too close to another car and heard them hurling profanity at me as I sped by; I’ve drifted a little close to people in the lane next to me, then watched as drivers would momentarily sway before regaining control or completely lose it and start a pile-up that ended in a truck exploding. I’ve been minding my own business when thrill-seekers pull up next to me and invite me to race. The city isn’t static, either, so the roads may undergo work–creating detours or slowing down traffic–which, when you’re trying to make a getaway after sticking up a bodega, can make for a very anticlimactic end to a petty criminal endeavor.

There’s plenty to marvel at on foot, too. There is a shocking number of building interiors in GTA 6, and they all feel carefully constructed. This isn’t a situation where a handful of buildings were made and then reused in slightly different configurations. Everything feels bespoke, so you can go to clothing stores where both the brick-and-mortar footprint and store inventories are unlike those of other stores, even those within the same chain. The same goes for salons, which have different specialty styles for hair, nails, and makeup. The type of tattoos you can get vary depending on the parlor and its location. There are no generic or repeated salons, cafes, banks, restaurants, pawn shops, gun stores, or garages. If any two stores look the same, I didn’t see them, which is impressive in its own way.

Everything looks impeccable, whether it’s the glass towers reflecting the bright lights of Vice City as the last rays of light peek over the rooftops; grimy back alleys with addicts slumped up against dirty walls or menacing gang members waiting for you to pass by; the muddy marshes where alligators lurk that’ll make you want to immediately head home to take a shower (yes, you can manually do that too); or the little towns on the outskirts that are run down and forgotten. I didn’t find a low-quality texture the entire time I was playing, and I was looking; text on leaflets, posters, billboards, and magazines are crystal clear.

Even among the people in Leonida, I never got the feeling that models had been reused to populate the map. Everyone I met looked like they were crafted to be seen, with voice lines and a look that stands up to scrutiny. Everyone in Leonida has main-character energy and, frankly, it’s absurd.

No matter where you look or what you look at, there’s a ubiquitous level of polish that should be impossible to achieve. But GTA 6 does, refusing to cut corners or make compromises, and it remains stunning throughout as a result. Light bounces off cars dynamically, you can look into windows and mirrors in buildings, car interiors are fully modeled, raising a bottle to drink will slowly deplete the fluid inside realistically, Lucia’s hair moves naturally as she runs her hands through it, Jason and Lucia will develop a patina of sweat after working out. And, on a PlayStation 5 Pro, it all looks fantastic.

Rockstar’s visual style looks brilliant on PS5

GTA 6’s visual fidelity is exemplary and I didn’t have any noticeable issues with performance outside of some extreme six-star wanted-level situations. As a large number of police pursued me, there was the occasional dip in frames. But for the most part, I didn’t have to think about that aspect of its technical performance.

However, some people may take issue with how it feels to move around the world. Running at 30 frames per second plays a big part in allowing the game to have the richness in detail that it does at its unprecedented scale, but some technical-minded people may question its responsiveness, particularly in a game that has so many points of interaction. I never felt like the framerate was a detriment. That may be because I’m attuned to the feel of 30fps, but I distinctly remember that slowness in responses being something I battled with in Red Dead Redemption 2.

In GTA 6 the intentionally designed slower, heavier, and more involved animation style brings weight to your presence in each environment, as it did Red Dead Redemption 2, but it isn’t as frustrating. This is partially because it is slightly quicker overall, but also because there’s no need to slowly pick things up and inspect everything as in Red Dead. GTA 6’s deliberate approach to its animations strengthens the simulation by enhancing the feeling of being in the world and affecting it more so than in Red Dead Redemption 2.

And when it matters, neither Jason nor Lucia’s life of crime is hampered by how they move. This far into a review, you’d think that the actual crux of the gameplay would be more of a focus, but like everything else, it is an extension of what came before it, whether that’s doing side missions, fun excursions, or heists.

After a dramatic opening setting up Jason and Lucia’s relationship, the duo resolve to fight their way to a better life. Both characters are playable and, although that means the character-select wheel has fewer options than GTA 5, you can similarly swap between them freely, outside of specific scenarios. When doing so, the camera pulls back to a satellite view of Leonida, then zeroes in on the character you’ve selected in a feat of tech that remains novel, even 13 years after seeing it in GTA 5. And, similarly to GTA 5, since Jason and Lucia both live their own lives when apart, you may drop in on them hanging out at home watching TV, or mid-workout at a gym, or being accosted by a strung-out fiend.

Most of the main missions require Lucia and Jason to be together and, in these cases, you can text the other character from the map to tell them to meet, or let the game bring them in naturally. It’s surprising how smoothly the game does this, often seeming like it was part of the actual cutscene. They don’t just pop up: sometimes a mission will begin and then the other person will walk in and apologize for being late; other times, one will wait for the other outside, leaning up against a wall.

However, each character also has missions chains built around giving them their own unique experience of Leonida, often exploring a part of their personal life or history that the other may not be privy to or want to be part of. Jason, for example, has a military background and has a very casual demeanor, while also being pretty imposing when he wants to be. This is something that longtime friend Dre’Quan makes good use of as he attempts to build his own record label. Jason ends up going along for a ride that involves feuding with rival artists online and humiliating them offline, while also trying to secure a big break for Dre’s up-and-coming female group, The Dimez.

Lucia is a strong personality that keeps her eyes on the prize

Lucia, meanwhile, is the more ambitious of the two, constantly working toward their new life and putting herself in increasingly risky scenarios to realize it. At the same time, she has her own dramatic friends to deal with. Her pal Andy gets herself caught up in a dangerous vaping situation that takes some very unexpected turns, while Lucia’s mother, Mia, pursues her own ambitions in ways that sometimes outpace her daughter’s recklessness. Both individually and as a Bonnie-and-Clyde duo, Lucia and Jason are well-drawn characters, performed exceptionally. Each could carry a game on their own, but together they introduce a whole different kind of dynamic to GTA.

There’s an elegant system built around their interpersonal dynamics that shapes how they behave toward one another, which in turn changes the tone of the game. Admittedly, I didn’t forcefully develop animosity between them to see how their relationship changes, so I can’t speak to how that plays out. I ended up mostly only angering one or the other after accidentally leaving them behind on a job or not checking in through a text or call. For all of the pre-release talk of their relationship being optional, GTA 6 feels like it puts its thumb on the scale a little. The story seems to prefer the two have a close relationship, so I followed through on that. I spent time holding hands, sneaking in hugs and kisses whenever possible, doing activities together, hanging out chatting, watching TV, and everything else couples do. This meant that, throughout the game, Jason and Lucia’s faith in one another was rock-solid; they showed a lot of mutual affection and concern where appropriate; complimenting haircuts, pointing out when one was due a shower, and longingly discussing a better life. I suspect it would be very different if I focused on straining their relationship, but that will have to wait for the next 60-hour playthrough.

I really appreciated how normal Lucia and Jason are relative to previous GTA protagonists. Instead of having extreme personalities and caricatures to anchor the game, both characters are much more relatable people who maintain that normalcy even when they’re caught up in unbelievable situations. While there is a criminal-profile element to how they behave and are presented that can affect them, I tried my best to avoid carnage, opting instead to go the quiet route when possible and aiming to incapacitate instead of kill. Who these two protagonists are puts the people they encounter into much starker contrast, showing the extent of the greed and corruption that holds some people down while propping others up. Many of the people they meet embody the various vices and pitfalls that could derail their future.

The crown jewels of GTA 6’s gameplay experience are the major heist missions. There are five chapters in the game and, across them, Lucia and Jason get embroiled in increasingly outrageous schemes to climb the wealth and social ladder. Regardless of how risky the job is, Rockstar treats each mission like it’s the climax of a Michael Mann movie; the heists are plotted, framed, and enacted as such. Like in GTA 5, there’s usually a series of missions that act as different stages of a job, which means that there’s a slow build-up in investment as you move toward execution. The scenarios Rockstar sets up are always multidimensional, often involving characters who clash in personality. Motivations may be muddied or unclear, and Jason and Lucia are regularly asked to take leaps of faith that could jeopardize their future. There’s always a sense of tension that builds to a dramatic finale when the big day comes.

Smaller robberies and heists can be done together too

In their life of crime, everything that can go wrong usually does, creating ample opportunities to engage in cover-based shooting, which feels satisfying thanks to great feedback and ample use of Max Payne-style slow-mo for killshots. Being able to switch between two characters who are in completely different places doing different things enhances the cinematic flair. One minute you could be engaged in a high-speed chase trying to get away from authorities as Jason, before the action smoothly shifts to Lucia laying down covering fire from a chopper. The time it takes to switch depends on how far the characters are from each other and, during missions where they’re together, it is instantaneous. Leonida is a place replete with corrupt politicians, greedy gangsters, and reckless criminals, and Rockstar makes great use of all available options to craft missions that’ll get your heart racing.

The story itself is another part that sticks fairly close to classic GTA plots, but the characters who make it up are as weird and memorable as in previous games. There are hapless idiots and caricatures who serve as perfect fodder for parody and commentary–of which there is a fair amount; hopeful friends you come to care about; and lunatics who can be genuinely frightening. It’s a game of twists and turns instigated by a very memorable cast, led by protagonists who are learning just how desperate the American dream really is. Most importantly, the writing manages to feel contemporary, despite being a game 13 years in the making–there are very few dated expressions or references that feel out of place.

There are a dizzying number of other things in GTA 6 that are deserving of praise. The incredible soundtrack by Justice that delivers thumping electronic beats makes key missions feel like you’re playing Drive. Despite not being a mini-game type of person, I found myself diving for treasure frequently–there’s a serene atmosphere underwater that I enjoyed as a contrast to the madness above. If you’re looking for the weird stories, there’s a crypto stuntman, a dude trying to grow the biggest strawberries in the state using dangerous chemicals, and so many other oddities to find. I’ve been obsessed with Kaleidoscope, a radio station that only plays covers of hits done by obscure real-world artists from around the world. I was also thankful for the detailed map system that organizes points of interest, activities, and locations in a way that’s easy on the eyes. There are some absolutely bizarre adverts around the city, there are outstanding acting and performances throughout, and so much more.

But the nature of the game, in terms of how big it is and how much of it there is, means I haven’t seen it all. There’s so much that I know I missed–there are entirely different ways to tackle missions, character interactions, and tasks I’ve yet to do; and even a whole different way to play the game as Jason and Lucia. Even now, despite reaching the end, my map is littered with things to do, and I’m excited to do them. It doesn’t feel like open-world bloat, the type many other games have opted for in the 13 years between the most recent GTA entries. It all either feels meaningful or is enjoyable enough to spend hours on without regret.

Amid so much GTA 6 does so well, only two minor things stick out as being dips in the quality of my experience. The first happened any time I needed to drive fast to escape or catch up to a target. I’ve never been able to get the hang of car physics in GTA games and the driving requires a little more finesse than I’m capable of, especially at high speeds, so I ended up crashing a bunch. Thankfully, those crashes rarely resulted in a mission failure, as you can quickly reset crashed cars and pick back up. If a crash happens during a critical mission, the checkpointing quickly gets the action moving again. But driving ineptitude is a _me_ problem, I suspect.

Prepare some of the coolest set-pieces you’ve played

Generally, the driving model makes getting behind the wheel of different cars worthwhile. I ended up taking a liking to “acquiring” cars for someone as a money-making scheme, and so got a good amount of time driving all kinds of vehicles. Each felt appropriately floaty or weighty, with lighter supercars reaching blisteringly fast speeds but feeling more fragile with every mile per hour, while trucks felt heavy and cumbersome. The everyday sedan was an ideal middle-ground, being able to pick up speed without making it difficult to handle–perfect for drifting around corners. The second issue is that there are several moments in the story when you’ll need to grind to earn a certain amount of money to progress, which I found frustrating, but which was ultimately a minor inconvenience.

Hours later, the fly that buzzed past Jason’s ear remains a peculiar but apt emblem of Grand Theft Auto 6’s achievements. Only Rockstar commands the time and money necessary to make a game as dedicated to small details. And only Rockstar can execute on the realising such a granular vision unimpeachably. The obsession with the minutia both explains and justifies the 13-year wait. This game is the culmination of everything the developer has previously achieved in the open-world space that pushes the genre to heights that, in all likelihood, no other developer will be able to reach. GTA 6 is an astonishing open-world action game that I’d wager won’t be outdone until Grand Theft Auto 7.
